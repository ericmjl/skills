# Advocacy, Cadences, and Objections

Eric-calibrated principles for persuasive content beyond the core framework:
advocacy blog posts, recurring post cadences, and handling price objections.
Load this file when the copy must convince rather than only describe, when a
promo recurs weekly, or when a reader asks "why does it cost so much?"

## Persona-matched CTAs

The close should propose a NEXT ACTION the specific reader persona can
plausibly take — not a generic "try this method today" call. Match the ask to
what that reader can actually do next.

- For a **lab scientist / lab group head**: the plausible action is rarely
  "install this library and code it yourself." It is "find a statistician to
  collaborate with," "send this post to your biostatistics core," or "raise
  it at your next methods meeting." Point at the human/organizational move.
- For a **developer**: "clone the repo and run the quickstart" is plausible.
- For a **manager**: "share this with your team's tech lead" is plausible.

A CTA the reader cannot act on is wasted closing real estate. Ask: "what is
the smallest plausible next step THIS reader can take?" and name it. Do not
pretend the reader will go off and build it solo.

## Rhetorical questions (reader self-insertion)

A rhetorical question converts a benefit claim into a FELT experience.
Instead of asserting "this saves you hours," ask: "How would you feel about
that instead of spending your afternoon doing it by hand?" The reader answers
in their own head and arrives at the benefit themselves.

- Place the question right after the concrete proof of the benefit.
- Frame it as a stark contrast between the NEW way (just demonstrated) and
  the OLD pain (the thing they still do by hand).
- Keep it to ONE sentence. A second question dilutes the first.

## End strong, no trailing hedges

The closing should land the thesis with punch — a short, declarative sentence
the reader carries away. Do NOT trail off into qualifications, "future work"
caveats, or hedging.

- **Legitimate qualifications** (a known limitation the reader will hit) →
  keep, but place mid-body, never in the closing.
- **Hedging reflexes** ("of course, this might not apply in all cases...") →
  cut. If the claim feels too strong, strengthen the EVIDENCE, do not soften
  the CLAIM.
- Cut the final paragraph if it dilutes the punch.

## Value-on-top-of-persuasion (recurring cadences)

The core framework governs a SINGLE piece of persuasive content. For a
RECURRING CADENCE — an 8-week LinkedIn series, weekly posts, a multi-issue
newsletter — pure emotional copy compounds into sales-fatigue. The reader who
saw the same fear button hammered in week 3 will not be there in week 7.

Structure each post in a recurring cadence as: **VALUE NUGGET (the "give") →
EMOTIONAL BRIDGE → CTA**.

- **Value nugget**: a genuinely portable, teachable idea drawn from the
  product's underlying thesis. Test: if the reader screenshots this one idea
  and sends it to a colleague with no context, do they get value?
- **Emotional bridge**: connect the nugget to the reader's pain or aspiration
  (problem-agitation plus rhetorical self-insertion).
- **CTA**: plausible and persona-matched (see above).

The value nugget does the persuasion work because it PROVES expertise — the
reader reasons, "if this is what they give away, the paid thing must be
deeper." Pure advocacy across a cadence erodes trust you cannot rebuild;
value-on-top-of-persuasion earns the right to pitch, week after week.

## Price objections: reframe, never itemize

When a reader asks "why does it cost so much?", NEVER justify the price with a
cost breakdown (itemized components, "it covers X and Y", staffing claims).
Itemizing invites line-item negotiation and reads defensive. Instead REFRAME
the price as an investment against long-horizon returns: what the capability
yields over years if applied even once (a career pivot, a role previously
written off, a skill that compounds) — "you're not buying the week, you're
buying the next decade of capability."

Rules:

- Keep ROI at the horizon level; never promise specific earnings figures or
  income guarantees.
- Never invent business facts (staffing levels, "full-time dedication",
  operational claims) as price justification — anchor every factual claim
  about the business to verified facts.

## When to hold back

"Marketing copy" does not mean aggressive sales-bro energy. It means:
structure the argument so the reader arrives at your conclusion because the
benefits are unmistakable, not because you shouted. In a technical blog post,
the persuasion should feel like a well-argued essay — these techniques shape
the STRUCTURE (problem-first, benefit-led, strong close), while the substance
stays rigorous and honest.

## Training: copy work

The fastest way to build taste is to copy great work by hand. Find copy that
converted or writing that is critically acclaimed in your genre. Transcribe
it word for word for weeks or months. Musicians learn by copying songs before
writing originals; writers learn by copying sentences before writing their
own. Do this with classic ads, landing pages, email sequences, or fiction.
Writing it by hand (not typing) improves retention and feel for rhythm.
