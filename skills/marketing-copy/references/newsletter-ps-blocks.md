# Newsletter P.S. Blocks (Event and Product Promotion)

A P.S. appended to a newsletter, Substack, or LinkedIn post is a mini-ad read
by skimmers — it must carry the full persuasion arc on its own for skimmers
AND tie into the main content of the post above it (bridge rule below).
Distilled from a friend-edited retreat-promotion P.S. that fixed what earlier
drafts were "still missing."

## When to use

- Promoting an event, workshop, retreat, course, or product at the bottom of a
  newsletter, Substack post, LinkedIn post, or blog email.
- Weekly promo cadences where the same offer recurs and the P.S. must stay
  fresh (see rotation guidance below).

This governs PROMOTIONAL P.S. blocks on published posts. Personal
correspondence P.S. lines follow normal letter conventions instead.

## Tie-in with the main post (the bridge rule)

A promotional P.S. must BRIDGE from the post it is attached to: the hook
derives from the post's own closing argument (what the post actually taught
or argued), not from a generic event plug that could sit under any post.
Eric (2026-09-12): "linkedin's P.S. needs to tie in better with the main
content. same goes for substack."

- Mine the post for the bridge. The technique or idea the post demonstrated
  becomes the P.S. hook ("This trick is an example of a tool that
  travels..."), so the promo reads as the post's natural last move rather
  than an ad interlude.
- Ground the bridge in the author's REAL bio facts. The honest version is
  usually the stronger version: "came out of my statistics training" was
  both wrong and weaker than the accurate "I taught myself Bayesian
  thinking in graduate school" — which is also the event's literal premise.
- Keep cross-surface consistency: the P.S. story must match what the linked
  post or landing page already says about the author and the event.

## The 5-part P.S. formula

Five parts, in order. Parts 1, 3, and 4 do the persuasion work; do not cut
them for length.

### 1. Demand-framed hook

"A lot of you have asked me: [benefit question in the reader's words]."

Social-proof opening: the offer answers EXISTING demand rather than
cold-selling. The question carries the benefit (speed, universality, the
reader's desired outcome), not the product name.

### 2. Credibility-anchored answer

"[Co-organizer], a [title] at [institution], and I are organizing [event] to
[benefit]."

Credentials and affiliation woven inline; no separate bio section.

### 3. Time-compression promise

"We will teach you in a week what took me 10 years to learn — from [starting
point] to [end state]."

The short-time vs long-time contrast IS the offer. Pair it with the author's
concrete before/after (lab bench to application).

### 4. Gratitude plus scarcity close

"Thank you to the many of you who have already signed up within the first
month — only a limited number of spots remain of the [20] curated attendees."

Double social proof (others signed up) plus scarcity against a fixed curated
cap. Curation signals selectivity, not just shortage. THIS is the part early
drafts were missing: without it the P.S. is a cold announcement; with it the
P.S. has momentum.

### 5. Plain details footer

Event name "(with [names])", application URL, dates, location. One per line,
no persuasion — just logistics. "Location: To be revealed soon" doubles as a
curiosity hook for the next issue.

## Worked example

> P.S. A lot of you have asked me: how do I learn to use agents to do
> anything, 10x faster, in any domain that you work in? Daniel Chen, a Data
> Science educator at the University of British Columbia, and I are
> organizing a Learn Anything with AI Retreat to equip you to make your jump
> faster. We will teach you in a week what it took me 10 years to learn —
> from the lab bench to application.
>
> Thank you to the many of you who have already signed up within the first
> month — we have a limited number of spots left of the 20 curated attendees.
> Details below:
>
> Learn Anything with AI (with Eric and Daniel)
>
> Applications open to waitlist first: <https://example.com/apply>
>
> Dates: Feb 15-19, 2027
>
> Location: To be revealed soon

## Adapting friend-edited or LLM drafts

Keep the structure; fix the grammar. Hand-edited drafts carry typos that a
verbatim reuse would propagate ("what it look myself 10 years" → "what it
took me 10 years to learn"; "a limited spots left" → "a limited number of
spots left"; "thank you for many of you" → "thank you to the many of you").

## Weekly rotation guidance

For a recurring cadence, keep parts 1, 3, and 4 constant (they carry the
persuasion). Rotate:

- Part 1's asked-question framing (different reader benefit each week).
- The details footer as news lands (location reveal, agenda, early-bird
  deadlines, speaker announcements).

Do not rotate the scarcity line into hype: real signups, real cap, real
deadlines only. Honor every limit you state.
